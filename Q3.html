From Physics to Everything

How a tool for scientists became the place you spend your whole day

Then: A Fix for Messy Research

In 1989, physicists at CERN had a problem every group project knows: everyone's work was saved somewhere different, and nobody could find anything. Tim Berners-Lee's fix was linked documents, so a scientist could click from one paper straight to another. It was built for a few thousand researchers sharing physics data.

Now: Literally Everything

Today, billions of people use that same idea to binge shows, order takeout, submit assignments at 11:59 p.m., and doomscroll through images that you can't tell are actually AI slop. The web went from serious science to the place we work, shop, learn, and procrastinate.

What Stayed the Same

Under all the videos and apps, the web still runs on what Berners-Lee built: HTML, URLs, and HTTP. And the core goal hasn't changed: connecting information so anyone can find it. Every link you click is a tiny version of his original idea.

What Changed

His web was plain text for a small group of experts. Ours is full of images, ads, and algorithms, used by almost everyone on Earth. That growth brought problems he never planned for, like misinformation and privacy concerns. Not bad for something that started as a way to organize physics papers.
